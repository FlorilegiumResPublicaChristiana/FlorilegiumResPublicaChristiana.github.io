Digest 1.1.10 (Ulpian): what justice is

Iustitia est constans et perpetua voluntas ius suum cuique tribuendi. Iuris praecepta sunt haec: honeste vivere, alterum non laedere, suum cuique tribuere. Iuris prudentia est divinarum atque humanarum rerum notitia, iusti atque iniusti scientia.

Key terms: tick each one your translation gets right.

Yours

Mike judged 10/10

Justice is the constant and perpetual will to assign to each person his due. The precepts of law are these: to live honourably, not to harm another, and to assign to each person his due. The prudence of law is the knowledge of divine and human matters, and the science of what is just and what is unjust.

The edition

Justice is a constant, unfailing disposition to give every one his legal due. The principles of law are these: Live uprightly, injure no man, give every man his due. To be learned in the law (jurisprudentia) is to be acquainted with divine and human things, to know what is just and what is unjust.

C. H. Monro, The Digest of Justinian, vol. 1 (Cambridge, 1904)

Digest 1.1.5 (Hermogenian): what the law of nations brought

Ex hoc iure gentium introducta bella, discretae gentes, regna condita, dominia distincta, agris termini positi, aedificia collocata, commercium, emptiones venditiones, locationes conductiones, obligationes institutae: exceptis quibusdam quae iure civili introductae sunt.

Key terms: tick each one your translation gets right.

Yours

Mike judged 10/10

From this law of nations, wars have been introduced, separate nations, established kingdoms, distinct dominions, boundaries set in the fields, buildings placed, commerce, purchases and sales, leases and hires, obligations instituted — except for certain things which have been introduced by civil law.

The edition

It was by this same jus gentium that war was introduced, nations were distinguished, kingdoms were established, rights of ownership were ascertained, boundaries were set to domains, buildings were erected, mutual traffic, purchase and sale, letting and hiring and obligations in general were set on foot, with the exception of a few of these last which were introduced by the civil law.

C. H. Monro, The Digest of Justinian, vol. 1 (Cambridge, 1904)

Digest 1.1.9 (Gaius): civil law and the law of nations

Omnes populi, qui legibus et moribus reguntur, partim suo proprio, partim communi omnium hominum iure utuntur. nam quod quisque populus ipse sibi ius constituit, id ipsius proprium civitatis est vocaturque ius civile, quasi ius proprium ipsius civitatis: quod vero naturalis ratio inter omnes homines constituit, id apud omnes peraeque custoditur vocaturque ius gentium, quasi quo iure omnes gentes utuntur.

Key terms: tick each one your translation gets right.

Yours

Mike judged 10/10

All peoples, who are governed by laws and customs, use partly their own, partly the common right of all mankind. For the law which each people establishes for itself is called the civil law, as being the proper law of that people; but the law which natural reason establishes among all mankind is equally observed by all and is called the law of nations, as being the law by which all nations make use.

The edition

All nations which are governed by statutes and customs make use partly of law which is peculiar to the respective nations, and partly of such as is common to all mankind. Whatever law any nation has established for itself is peculiar to the particular state (civitas), and is called civil law, as being the peculiar law of that state, but law which natural reason has laid down for mankind in general is maintained equally by all men, and is called jus gentium, as being the law which all nations use.

C. H. Monro, The Digest of Justinian, vol. 1 (Cambridge, 1904)

Justinian, Institutes 1.9: the father's power

In potestate nostra sunt liberi nostri, quos ex iustis nuptiis procreaverimus. Nuptiae autem sive matrimonium est viri et mulieris coniunctio, individuam consuetudinem vitae continens. Ius autem potestatis quod in liberos habemus proprium est civium Romanorum: nulli enim alii sunt homines qui talem in liberos habeant potestatem qualem nos habemus. Qui igitur ex te et uxore tua nascitur, in tua potestate est: item qui ex filio tuo et uxore eius nascitur, id est nepos tuus et neptis, aeque in tua sunt potestate, et pronepos et proneptis et deinceps ceteri. qui tamen ex filia tua nascitur, in tua potestate non est, sed in patris eius.

Key terms: tick each one your translation gets right.

Yours

Mike judged 9/10

Our descendants are in our power, those whom we have begotten through just emissaries. Marriage, however, or matrimony, is the union of a man and a woman, containing an individual custom of life. The right of power that we have over our descendants is proper to Roman citizens: for no other men are there who have such power over their descendants as we have. Therefore, whoever is born of you and your wife is in your power: likewise, whoever is born of your son and his wife, that is, your grandson and granddaughter, are equally in your power, and your great-grandson and great-granddaughter, and henceforward all others. Whoever, however, is born of your daughter is not in your power, but in that of her father.

The edition

Our children whom we have begotten in lawful wedlock are in our power. Wedlock or matrimony is the union of male and female, involving the habitual intercourse of daily life. The power which we have over our children is peculiar to Roman citizens, and is found in no other nation. The offspring then of you and your wife is in your power, and so too is that of your son and his wife, that is to say, your grandson and granddaughter, and so on. But the offspring of your daughter is not in your power, but in that of its own father.

J. B. Moyle, The Institutes of Justinian (Oxford, 1883)
