Vision

Where modern law comes from

Property, possession, sovereignty, office, fidelity, privilege, the rule that a judge must hear both sides: these ideas did not begin with modern constitutions and civil codes. Many of them were worked out first in Roman law, then reshaped by canonists, glossators, papal chanceries and royal courts between the twelfth and the fourteenth centuries. The words were Latin, and the arguments were made in thousands of documents that few people now read.

This platform reads them. It is a source-grounded tool for tracing legal concepts from Roman and medieval law toward the present, and for showing, with words and numbers, where modern law comes from and where it broke with its past.

Words and numbers

Every concept is followed in two ways at once.

  • Words. What a term meant, who used it, in what kind of document, and how its meaning shifted. Each reading rests on the passage itself, which can always be opened and checked.
  • Numbers. How often a term, formula or clause appears, in which period and region, and how its frequency changes. Measured against a fixed base of records, a rise or fall becomes evidence rather than impression.

Together they show both continuity and rupture. Some concepts run almost unbroken from Roman law into the modern codes; others were rebuilt in the early modern period and only borrowed the old vocabulary. The tool does not assume that modern law descends from the medieval. It tests it, one concept at a time.

Traceability

Every claim on the platform can be followed back to its source: the document, the edition, the passage. Counts can be rerun. Readings can be compared with a second reader. Nothing rests on an unreferenced summary. This is what makes the tool useful to scholars, and what makes it safe to teach with.

Mike

At the centre is Mike, a reader trained on medieval Latin legal texts. Mike works the way a medieval Latin scholar works: from the significations of a word, to the dictionary, to the context of the passage, and only then to a conclusion. Its reasoning is written out, so that a student or researcher can see not only the answer but how it was reached, and where it might be wrong.

Mike is the first of a family. Each reader will be trained on the legal language of its own period and will capture how that period thought: the lawyers of Rome, the canonists and glossators, the jurists of the early modern codes, the drafters of modern constitutions. Placed side by side, they let a concept be read as each age understood it, rather than through the eyes of our own.

The first case study

The method was built and tested on the charters and laws of medieval Hungary and Transylvania under the Árpád and Angevin kings, compared with England, Sicily and the Holy See. That corpus was chosen because the material was at hand, not because the tool is limited to it. The studies published here, and the book The Outside King, show what the method can do on a single kingdom. The same approach applies to any body of legal sources.

Where it is going

  • Forward in time. From Roman and medieval law to the early modern codes and on to modern law, concept by concept, always from official and public sources.
  • Teaching. Courses in the history of law, the philosophy of law and concept tracing, in which students learn to read a source, build an argument from it and cite it properly.
  • Research. A tool researchers can put questions to and receive answers grounded in the sources, with the passages shown.

The aim is a better understanding of the modern legal world by knowing, precisely and verifiably, how it was made.
