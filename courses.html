Courses from the sources to modern law · in preparation

Eight courses taught from the sources with the platform's tools. Each session reads a Roman or medieval text, with its dictionaries and translations, and then follows the concept into modern law. Click a course to see its sessions. Format and dates will be announced here.

Foundations

IHistory of law Legal concepts, then and nowSix concepts of private and procedural law, from the Roman and medieval sources into the civil codes.6 sessions

For students of law and history who want to know where the concepts of their codes come from.

Six concepts of private and procedural law, each read in its Roman or medieval sources and then followed into the modern civil codes. The last session teaches the platform's method, so that students can trace a concept of their own.

  1. Ownership and possession

    Then: the sourcesdominium and possessio; the king's order to put a grantee into possession
    Now: the modern conceptproperty and the protection of possession in the civil codes
    Study: Possessio

  2. Contract

    Then: the sourcesthe Roman contracts; the canonists' pacta quantumcumque nuda servanda sunt (X 1.35.1)
    Now: the modern conceptpacta sunt servanda, good faith and freedom of contract

  3. Persons and the family

    Then: the sourcespatria potestas (Institutes 1.9); marriage as individua consuetudo vitae
    Now: the modern conceptparental authority, marriage and legal capacity

  4. Grant, gift and inheritance

    Then: the sourcesthe perpetual grant, donatio and hereditas in the royal charters
    Now: the modern conceptownership in perpetuity, gifts and succession
    Study: In Perpetuum, Donatio, Hereditas

  5. Courts, witnesses and proof

    Then: the sourcestestes, inquisitio and sententia; hearing both sides
    Now: the modern conceptdue process and the law of evidence
    Study: Witnesses, Inquisitio, Sententia

  6. Trace a concept yourself

    Then: the sourcesfind a concept's words, count them across the sources, read the cases
    Now: the modern concepta short study of one modern legal concept and its history
IIPhilosophy of law Jurisprudence: what law is and who makes itJustice, natural law, the will of the prince and the rule of law, as the jurists first argued them.6 sessions

For students of law, philosophy and politics.

The great questions of legal theory as the Roman jurists and the medieval lawyers first put them: what justice is, where law comes from, whether the ruler is above the law, and who must consent to it. Each session ends with the modern debate the old texts opened.

  1. Justice and the science of law

    Then: the sourcesIustitia est constans et perpetua voluntas ius suum cuique tribuendi (D. 1.1.10)
    Now: the modern concepttheories of justice; law as a discipline

  2. Natural law, the law of nations, civil law

    Then: the sourcesUlpian, Gaius and Hermogenian (D. 1.1.1–9)
    Now: the modern conceptnatural rights, human rights and international law

  3. The will of the prince

    Then: the sourcesquod principi placuit, legis habet vigorem (D. 1.4.1); princeps legibus solutus (D. 1.3.31)
    Now: the modern conceptsovereignty, from Bodin (1576) to the modern state

  4. The ruler bound by law

    Then: the sourcesdigna vox (C. 1.14.4); John of Salisbury's Policraticus (1159)
    Now: the modern conceptthe rule of law and constitutional limits

  5. Two powers

    Then: the sourcesplenitudo potestatis: papal and royal fullness of power
    Now: the modern conceptthe separation of powers; church and state
    Study: Plenitudo Potestatis

  6. What touches all

    Then: the sourcesquod omnes tangit ab omnibus approbari debet (C. 5.59.5)
    Now: the modern conceptconsent, representation and parliaments
IIIEuropean legal history The common law of Europe: ius communeOne learned law for five centuries, from Bologna to the national codes and European law today.6 sessions

For students of European law and comparative law.

For five centuries Europe shared one learned law, Roman and canon law taught in the universities and applied beside local custom. The course follows it from Bologna to the national codes, and asks what remains of it in European law today.

  1. The Digest rediscovered

    Then: the sourcesBologna, Irnerius and the glossators; the Glossa ordinaria of Accursius
    Now: the modern conceptthe university and the professional lawyer

  2. Canon law, a law for all Christendom

    Then: the sourcesGratian's Decretum (c. 1140), the decretals, Hostiensis' Summa aurea (1253)
    Now: the modern conceptprocedure, marriage and corporations in modern law

  3. The commentators

    Then: the sourcesBartolus and Baldus: the law of cities and kingdoms
    Now: the modern conceptconflict of laws and the legal opinion

  4. Rex in regno suo imperator

    Then: the sourceskingdoms take up the common law; Hungary and Werbőczy's Tripartitum (1514)
    Now: the modern conceptnational law within a shared tradition
    Study: Banus, The palatine

  5. Codification

    Then: the sourcesthe Code civil (1804), the Austrian ABGB (1811), the German BGB (1900)
    Now: the modern conceptthe common law becomes national law

  6. A new ius commune?

    Then: the sourcesthe shared Roman and canon foundations
    Now: the modern conceptEuropean Union law and the harmonisation of private law

Into modern law

IVConstitutional law Sovereignty and the constitutional stateFrom the king's independence and the Golden Bull to constitutions, parliaments and fundamental rights.6 sessions

For students of constitutional law, political science and history.

How a medieval kingdom limited its king and how those limits became the parts of a modern constitution: sovereignty, a right of resistance, representation, ministers, rights and their confirmation.

  1. The king's independence

    Then: the sourcesrex in regno suo imperator est: no superior in temporal matters
    Now: the modern conceptthe sovereign state and its recognition

  2. Limits and resistance

    Then: the sourcesthe Golden Bull of 1222 and its clause allowing resistance to a king who breaks it
    Now: the modern conceptconstitutional guarantees and the right of resistance (German Basic Law, art. 20(4))

  3. Representation

    Then: the sourcesquod omnes tangit and the assemblies of the realm
    Now: the modern conceptparliaments and the representation of the people

  4. The king's officers

    Then: the sourcesthe palatine, the count of the county, the ban
    Now: the modern conceptministers, the executive and local government
    Study: The palatine, The county count, Banus

  5. Liberty and privilege

    Then: the sourceslibertas and privilegium in the royal charters
    Now: the modern conceptfundamental rights, and rights for some against rights for all
    Study: Libertas, Privilegium

  6. Confirmation

    Then: the sourceseach new king confirms the privileges of his predecessors
    Now: the modern conceptconstitutional continuity and amendment
    Study: Confirmatio
VCriminal law and procedure Criminal justice and the fair trialFrom the ordeal and the inquisition to evidence, appeal and the right to a fair trial.6 sessions

For students of criminal law and procedure, and practitioners.

The medieval courts invented much of the modern trial: the investigation, the witness, the written sentence, the appeal, and the rule that the other side must be heard. The course reads their records and sets them beside the guarantees of a fair trial today.

  1. Ordeal and oath

    Then: the sourcesthe ordeal and its end: clergy forbidden to take part (Fourth Lateran Council, 1215)
    Now: the modern conceptrational proof and the burden of proof

  2. The inquiry

    Then: the sourcesinquisitio: the judge's own investigation
    Now: the modern conceptinvestigation and the public prosecutor
    Study: Inquisitio

  3. Witnesses

    Then: the sourcestestes: who may testify, and how testimony is weighed
    Now: the modern conceptthe law of evidence and cross-examination
    Study: Witnesses

  4. Judgment and appeal

    Then: the sourcessententia and the appeal to a higher judge
    Now: the modern conceptthe reasoned judgment and the right of appeal
    Study: Sententia

  5. Sanctions

    Then: the sourcesexcommunication and the ban of the realm
    Now: the modern conceptpunishment and its limits
    Study: Excommunicatio

  6. Hear the other side

    Then: the sourcesaudiatur et altera pars; the canonists' presumption that a person is innocent until proved guilty
    Now: the modern conceptthe right to a fair trial (European Convention on Human Rights, art. 6)
VIPublic international law International law and human rightsFrom the ius gentium, legates and just war to diplomacy, the laws of war and human rights.6 sessions

For students of international law, international relations and human rights.

The law between peoples began as the Roman ius gentium and grew in the medieval law of envoys, war and the freedom of persons. The course follows it to Grotius and to the human rights instruments of the twentieth century.

  1. The law of nations

    Then: the sourcesius gentium in the Digest (D. 1.1.5, 1.1.9)
    Now: the modern conceptinternational law and its sources

  2. Envoys

    Then: the sourcesthe papal legate and his protection
    Now: the modern conceptdiplomacy and diplomatic immunity
    Study: Legatus

  3. Just war

    Then: the sourcesGratian's Decretum, Causa 23
    Now: the modern conceptthe laws of war, from Grotius (1625) to the Geneva Conventions

  4. Natural rights

    Then: the sourcesthe canonists' ius naturale
    Now: the modern conceptthe Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

  5. Free and unfree

    Then: the sourceslibertas: freedmen, servants and the free
    Now: the modern conceptpersonal freedom and the abolition of servitude
    Study: Libertas

  6. Church and kingdom

    Then: the sourcesthe two powers and the freedom of the Church
    Now: the modern conceptfreedom of religion and the secular state
    Study: Plenitudo Potestatis
VIICommercial and financial law Money, trade and the corporationUsury, rents, tolls, tithes and arbitration, and the companies, credit and taxes they led to.6 sessions

For students of commercial, financial and tax law, and economists.

Medieval lawyers had to decide when lending was lawful, how a rent could be sold, who could levy a toll and how a body of people could act as one. Their answers shaped interest, credit, taxation, arbitration and the company.

  1. Usury

    Then: the sourcesusura and the lending contract
    Now: the modern conceptinterest and consumer credit law
    Study: Usura

  2. Rents

    Then: the sourcescensus: the sale of a yearly rent
    Now: the modern conceptannuities and secured credit
    Study: Census

  3. Tolls and taxes

    Then: the sourcestributa: who may levy a toll
    Now: the modern concepttaxation and customs duties
    Study: Tributa

  4. The tithe

    Then: the sourcesdecima and nona
    Now: the modern conceptchurch tax and public finance
    Study: Decima and Nona

  5. The corporation

    Then: the sourcesuniversitas: many people acting as one body
    Now: the modern conceptlegal personality and the company

  6. Arbitration

    Then: the sourcesarbitrium: disputes settled by chosen judges
    Now: the modern conceptarbitration and mediation today
    Study: Arbitrium
VIIILegal language Legal Latin for today's lawyersThe Latin still used in courts and codes, and how to read a legal source with the platform's tools.6 sessions

For law students and practitioners who meet Latin in judgments, codes and textbooks.

Courts still decide ultra vires, cite res iudicata and apply pacta sunt servanda. The course explains these terms from their sources and teaches students to read and translate a legal text in Latin with the dictionaries, the glossary and Mike.

  1. Maxims still in use

    Then: the sourcespacta sunt servanda, res iudicata, audiatur et altera pars
    Now: the modern concepthow courts use them today

  2. Terms of art

    Then: the sourcesbona fides, in rem and in personam, ultra vires
    Now: the modern concepttheir meaning in modern codes and judgments

  3. Roman words in the codes

    Then: the sourcespossessio, obligatio, usufructus, servitus
    Now: the modern conceptthe vocabulary of the civil codes

  4. Reading a charter

    Then: the sourcesthe parts of a royal charter and their formulas
    Now: the modern conceptgloss reading on the platform

  5. Translating with the tools

    Then: the sourcesdictionaries, the period glossary and Mike's translation
    Now: the modern conceptusing AI help critically

  6. Your translation, judged

    Then: the sourcesa passage of your choice
    Now: the modern conceptTest yourself and the seminar
